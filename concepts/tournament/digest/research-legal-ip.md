# legal-ip: Third-party IP (TV/film characters, parody, art-style, soundalikes, app-name trademarks) in paid Apple App Store apps, as of September 2026

Apple's rules have not loosened. Guideline 5.2.1 still reads: "Don't use protected third-party material such as trademarks, copyrighted works, or patented ideas in your app without permission, and don't include misleading, false, or copycat representations, names, or metadata." The two 2026 revisions (Feb 6 and June 8) touched 1.2, 4.3, 4.5.3 and the intro; 5.2 was untouched. Nov 2025 added 4.1(c): you cannot use another developer's icon, brand or product name in your icon or name. In practice App Review does not weigh fair use: when a reviewer sees content that "resembles" a third-party property, the only exits are "attach documentary evidence" of a license or remove the content. Apple's 2025 transparency report shows 495,673 "Legal" rejections and 1,177 apps removed for "DPLA 6.3 - Intellectual Property Infringement," plus 279 for "Copycats."

Post-publication risk is bigger than review risk. Apple's Content Dispute form lets any rights holder (up to 50 apps per claim, under penalty of perjury) file; Apple "will contact the provider of the disputed content ... and ask that they work with you directly," and removes the app if the claimant reports it "unresolved." TV Time (Nov 2024) was pulled over user-uploaded cover art even though the complainant produced no registration; Musi was removed in Sept 2024 and a federal judge refused to force reinstatement (Jan 9, 2025). Disney sent cease-and-desists to Character.AI and Google (2025) and ByteDance (Feb 2026) over unlicensed characters, and Marvel Characters, Inc. holds ~2,000 live US marks including AVENGERS in Class 9 (downloadable game software). Using named characters, likenesses, logos or quotes from Bob's Burgers, South Park, American Dad or the Avengers films in a paid app is not viable.

Parody is weaker than developers assume. Jack Daniel's v. VIP (2023, 9-0) removed Rogers/First-Amendment protection when a mark is used as your own source identifier; Warhol v. Goldsmith (2023) held a commercial use with a similar purpose fails factor one; Dr. Seuss v. ComicMix (9th Cir. 2020) held a Seuss/Star Trek mashup "merely mimicking Seuss' style without critiquing" was infringement; Walt Disney v. Air Pirates (1978) rejected parody for near-identical character drawings. Apple's only satire carve-out (1.1.1, "professional political satirists and humorists") concerns offensive content, not IP.

Lookalikes and soundalikes are independently dangerous: Krofft v. McDonald's found infringement from characters capturing the "total concept and feel" (and reusing the same voice supplier); Midler and Waits make deliberate voice imitation a right-of-publicity tort ($2.475M verdict). Apple rejected Kill the Plumber (a Mario parody) until its art was redrawn, removed Mole Kart on Nintendo's complaint, and now flags AI-generated icons that echo Mario/Sonic.

What is safe: art style itself. The Copyright Office's May 2025 report states style is something "copyright does not protect," with the caveat that style replication can still "capture protectible elements." Bold flat-color adult-animation aesthetics, ensemble-family comedy, and "assemble the team" stakes are ideas/genre; original characters, names, world, and music are protectable expression you would own. Suno's paid tiers grant commercial rights including "video games," but Suno warns this "does not guarantee copyright protection" and its ownership language shifted in 2026; Envato Elements is one license per end product, no as-is redistribution, and nothing built from an Item can be trademarked.

## Key facts (sourced)
- Guideline 5.2.1 (current text, Sept 2026): "Don't use protected third-party material such as trademarks, copyrighted works, or patented ideas in your app without permission, and don't include misleading, false, or copycat representations, names, or metadata in your app bundle or developer name. Apps should be submitted by the person or legal entity that owns or has licensed the intellectual property and other relevant rights."
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/
- Guideline 4.1 Copycats: (a) "Don't simply copy the latest popular app ... or make some minor changes to another app's name or UI and pass it off as your own"; (b) impersonating other apps/services is a Developer Code of Conduct violation that may result in removal from the Developer Program; (c) "You cannot use another developer's icon, brand, or product name in your app's icon or name, without approval from the developer."
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/
- 4.1(c) was added in the November 13, 2025 guideline update (one of nine changes, alongside 1.2.1(a), 2.5.10, 3.2.2(ix), 4.7, 4.7.2, 4.7.5, 5.1.1(ix), 5.1.2(i)).
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=ey6d8onl
- Guideline 2.3.7: "Choose a unique app name, assign keywords that accurately describe your app, and don't try to pack any of your metadata with trademarked terms, popular app names ... App names must be limited to 30 characters ... Apple may modify inappropriate keywords at any time."
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/
- Guideline 5.2.5 bars apps "confusingly similar to an existing Apple product, interface (e.g. Finder), app ... or advertising theme" and bars Apple emoji in apps, keyboards and Sticker packs; a developer was rejected under 5.2.5 for an app icon that merely resembled an iPhone.
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/698492
- The only satire carve-out in the guidelines is in 1.1.1 (objectionable content): "Professional political satirists and humorists are generally exempt from this requirement." It applies to offensiveness, not to intellectual property.
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/
- Guideline introduction: "We will reject apps for any content or behavior that we believe is over the line. What line, you ask? Well, as a Supreme Court Justice once said, 'I'll know it when I see it'." Section 5 preamble: "Apps must comply with all legal requirements in any location where you make them available (if you're not sure, check with a lawyer)."
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/
- 2026 guideline revisions: February 6, 2026 changed only 1.2 (random/anonymous chat is UGC).
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=d75yllv4
- June 8, 2026 revision changed the Introduction (kid/teen safety), 1.2, 4.3(a), 4.3(b) and 4.5.3 (Live Activities spam) plus multiple DPLA sections; section 5.2 was not changed.
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=a233fmpw
- New 4.3(b) (June 2026): "Certain kinds of apps, such as dating, flashlight, sound effects, wallpaper, simple timers, and fortune telling, are well established on the App Store and we will not accept new submissions unless they offer a meaningfully different or improved experience ... Other kinds of apps, such as drinking games, Kama Sutra, fart, and burp apps, are mediocre, low-quality, or low-effort and do not add value."
  Source: https://www.macrumors.com/2026/06/09/app-store-guidelines-low-quality-apps/
- Apple's 2025 App Store Transparency Report: 9,100,620 submissions reviewed, 2,093,244 rejected; "Legal" category accounted for 495,673 rejections; 166,899 apps removed; top removal provisions include "DPLA 6.3 - Intellectual Property Infringement" (1,177 apps) and "Guideline 4.1.0 - Copycats" (279 apps).
  Source: https://www.apple.com/legal/app-store/transparency/2025/
- Apple's App Store Content Dispute process: "Upon receiving your submission, we will contact the provider of the disputed content regarding your claim and ask that they work with you directly to resolve the issue." Apple does not adjudicate the merits.
  Source: https://www.apple.com/legal/intellectual-property/dispute-forms/app-store/

  Source: https://www.apple.com/legal/intellectual-property/dispute-forms/app-store/app-name-dispute.html

  Source: https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/20/tv-time-points-to-apples-significant-power-over-developers-after-being-removed-from-app-store

  Source: https://appleinsider.com/articles/24/11/20/apples-quick-app-store-takedowns-over-copyright-claims-are-a-nightmare-for-developers

  Source: https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/96814

  Source: https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/794173

Developers resolved it with signed authorization letters, a client-owned developer account, or (in one case) a 17-page appeal.
  Source: https://www.b4x.com/android/forum/threads/guideline-5-2-1-legal-intellectual-property.88236/
- Parody/fair use at App Review: a developer whose parody app 'Starship Launcher' was rejected as a copycat argued 17 U.S.C. §107 and Apple's 1.1.1 humorist exemption in a public forum post; App Review's only reply was "We believe we have resolved this issue," with no stated acceptance of the fair-use argument.
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/776202
- Long-standing developer-forum framing of fair use at Apple: "There are two issues - whether you fall under fair use and whether the App Store perceives you fall under fair use. The first is a matter for the courts to decide if and when you get sued by the copyright holders... The second is your immediate problem."
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/81365
- Kill the Plumber, a platformer parody of Super Mario Bros., was initially rejected by Apple as "potentially violating Nintendo's copyright"; "After the graphics were changed, Apple accepted it," shipping Feb 2, 2016 as Kill the Plumber World with artwork "less reminiscent of Nintendo's games."
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kill_the_Plumber
- Mole Kart (iOS/Android) was removed by Apple in early 2012 "due to a claim by Nintendo for ripping off Mario Kart"; it returned rebranded as Mole Kart I on May 4, 2012.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_Kart
- February 2014: Apple and Google began rejecting games with "Flappy" in the title; 'Flappy Dragon' was rejected for attempting to "leverage a popular app" under the then-guideline on "false, fraudulent, or misleading representations."
  Source: https://techcrunch.com/2014/02/15/apple-google-begin-rejecting-games-with-flappy-in-the-title/
- 2026 reviewer behavior on lookalikes: AI-generated icons prompted as a "cute red plumber" or "fast blue hedgehog mascot" that come out resembling Mario/Sonic are being rejected under 5.2.2/5.2 for resembling "protected third-party content"; the fix is to regenerate with visual primitives, scrub brand echoes from metadata, and confirm originality in Resolution Center (PTKD Journal, May 17, 2026).
  Source: https://ptkd.com/journal/app-store-rejection-5-2-2-third-party-ip-ai-icons
- Apple does not accept fair-use claims on the first round in practice; its standard remedy text is to "attach documentary evidence in the App Review Information section in App Store Connect" and "Once Legal has reviewed your documentation and confirms its validity, Apple will proceed with the review of your app."
  Source: https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/699048
- Jack Daniel's Properties v. VIP Products (U.S. Supreme Court, June 8, 2023, 9-0): "When an alleged infringer uses a trademark as a designation of source for the infringer's own goods, the Rogers test does not apply" — a parody used as your own brand/title gets no special First Amendment shield and goes straight to likelihood-of-confusion.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Daniel%27s_Properties,_Inc._v._VIP_Products_LLC
- Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (U.S. Supreme Court, May 18, 2023): where a commercial use shares substantially the same purpose as the original, the first fair-use factor weighs against the user even if the new work adds new expression; 'transformative' aesthetics alone no longer carry fair use.
  Source: https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/Andy-Warhol-Found-for-the-Visual-Arts-Inc-v-Goldsmith-143-S-Ct-1258-2023.pdf
- Dr. Seuss Enterprises v. ComicMix (9th Cir., Dec 18, 2020): a Seuss/Star Trek mashup was not a parody and not fair use because it "merely mimick[ed] Seuss' style without critiquing or commenting on that style," took Seussian elements "to get attention or to avoid the drudgery in working up something fresh," and would "curtail Seuss' ability to exploit the derivative works market."
  Source: https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2020/12/dr-seuss-enterprises-lp-v-comicmix-llc
- Walt Disney Productions v. Air Pirates (9th Cir. 1978): adult 'counter-culture' comics depicting 17+ Disney characters were not protected parody because the depictions were essentially indistinguishable from Disney's originals; the court said defendants could have made their point without confusingly similar depictions.
  Source: https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/waltdsney-airpirates-9thcir1978.pdf
- Sid & Marty Krofft v. McDonald's (9th Cir. 1977): McDonaldland characters infringed H.R. Pufnstuf by capturing the show's "total concept and feel" even though not identical; McDonald's agency had hired the Kroffts' former staff and the same voice supplier — lookalike-plus-soundalike ensembles are a documented losing pattern.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sid_%26_Marty_Krofft_Television_Productions_Inc._v._McDonald%27s_Corp.
- DC Comics v. Towle (9th Cir. 2015) character-copyright test: a character is protected if it (1) has physical as well as conceptual qualities, (2) is sufficiently delineated to be recognizable whenever it appears, and (3) is especially distinctive, not a stock character. Recognizable TV cartoon characters easily satisfy this.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC_Comics_v._Mark_Towle
- Soundalikes: Waits v. Frito-Lay (9th Cir. 1992) upheld $2.475 million for deliberately imitating Tom Waits' voice in an ad; under Midler v. Ford, "when a distinctive voice of a professional singer is widely known and is deliberately imitated in order to sell a product, the sellers have appropriated what is not theirs and have committed a tort."
  Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/90-55981/90-55981.html
- U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright and AI Part 3 (pre-publication, May 2025), p.69-70: commenters raised concerns about outputs "that imitate a creator's style, which copyright does not protect"; but "There may, however, be cases where the replication of 'style' does capture protectible elements of an original work of authorship."
  Source: https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf
- Practitioner analysis of 'Studio Ghibli-style' commercial use (Apr 24, 2025): style alone is not copyrightable, but reproducing actual characters/scenes risks statutory damages up to $150,000 per work, and "don't ... write anything that implies you are licensed by or otherwise affiliated with" the studio (unfair competition / false endorsement risk).
  Source: https://johnfarmer.substack.com/p/what-risk-does-your-business-face
- AM General v. Activision (S.D.N.Y., Mar 31, 2020): realistic Humvees in Call of Duty were protected expressive use under Rogers because they had artistic relevance and did not explicitly mislead — the rare case where a trademark inside a game's world (not its title/brand) survived.
  Source: https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/blogs/incontestable/in-legal-warfare-over-humvee-trademarks-the-first-amendment-goes-beyond-the-call-of-duty-in-dismissing-am-generals-claims.html
- AVENGERS is registered to Marvel Characters, Inc. in International Class 009 covering "Computer game software downloadable from a global computer network," "Downloadable computer game software via a global computer network and wireless devices," and "Video game software" (Justia listing, serial 85430708).
  Source: https://trademarks.justia.com/854/30/avengers-85430708.html
- Marvel Characters, Inc. holds 'AVENGERS: ENDGAME' (serial 88215862) and 'AVENGERS ASSEMBLE' registrations, and per TrademarkElite owns roughly 2,038 U.S. trademarks, with 'AVENGERS: SECRET WARS' filed as recently as September 1, 2026.
  Source: https://www.trademarkelite.com/trademark/trademark-owner/MARVEL%20CHARACTERS,%20INC.

  Source: https://www.trademarkia.com/south-park-85598415
- BOB'S BURGERS is registered to Twentieth Century Fox Film Corporation (Reg. 4254298, Dec 4, 2012) covering downloadable audio/video recordings and "downloadable graphics via a global computer network and wireless devices," plus a 2015 Class 28 games/toys application.
  Source: https://trademarks.justia.com/867/67/bob-s-86767969.html

## Implications for the concept
- Zero named IP, anywhere: no character names, likenesses, catchphrases, episode titles, show logos, or franchise words (Avengers, Endgame, Infinity War, South Park, Bob's Burgers, American Dad) in the app binary, name, subtitle, keywords, screenshots, description, or marketing. Apple's 2.3.7 rejection text targets metadata specifically, and AVENGERS is registered in Class 9 for downloadable game software, so even a keyword is a trademark problem.
- Capture tone, not characters. Ensemble-family comedy, cutting satire, and 'assemble the team / stakes are everything' energy are unprotectable ideas and genre; bold flat cartoon color is an unprotectable style (Copyright Office, May 2025). Build a fully original cast with distinct silhouettes, names, and a world that would not pass the Towle 'recognizable as the same character' test for any existing property.
- Avoid 'lookalike ensembles': do not design a family that maps 1:1 onto a known show's roster (dad/mom/two kids/talking pet, or four boys in a snowy town). Krofft v. McDonald's found infringement from 'total concept and feel' with non-identical characters; Kill the Plumber was blocked by Apple until its art stopped 'resembling' Nintendo's. Change the archetype mix, not just the drawing.
- No soundalike voices or 'in the style of' character impressions in Suno prompts or any voice lines. Midler/Waits make deliberate imitation of a distinctive known voice a tort, and the Krofft case flagged reuse of the original voice talent. Keep Suno prompts to genre/mood/instrumentation, never 'sounds like [artist/show theme]'.
- Do not rely on parody or fair use as the design basis. Jack Daniel's (2023) strips protection when the parody is your own brand/title; Warhol (2023) and Dr. Seuss v. ComicMix (2020) show commercial mashups that 'mimic style' without critiquing the original lose; Air Pirates shows near-copies of characters lose even in satire. App Review will not evaluate a fair-use argument at all; it asks for a license or removal.
- Assume post-launch takedown risk is asymmetric: any rights holder can file Apple's Content Dispute form and Apple removes on an 'unresolved' flag without weighing evidence (TV Time, 2024). For a solo developer that means designing so no plausible claimant exists, keeping a one-page 'originality dossier' (character sheets, dated source files, Suno generation logs, Envato license records) ready to attach in App Review Information.
- Name the app defensively: pick a coined or arbitrary name, run a USPTO TESS/TSDR knock-out search plus an App Store search in every localization, avoid any franchise word or near-spelling, and consider filing your own U.S. trademark application early. Apple's App Name Dispute process means a prior mark holder can block or force a rename.
- If the concept references pop culture at all, do it only as user-supplied or generic categories ('90s sitcoms', 'superhero movies') without depicting or naming works, and never state or imply affiliation. Referential compatibility language is allowed in descriptions but not in the name or icon.
- Because 4.3(b) (June 2026) now explicitly rejects sound-effect, wallpaper, and low-effort novelty apps unless 'meaningfully different,' a music/soundboard-style product must lead with a genuinely new mechanic or utility; the Suno library should be a feature inside a larger experience, not the product.
- Music rights hygiene: generate every track on an active Suno Pro/Premier subscription, export and archive dated files and generation IDs, and read the 2026 Suno terms before launch given the reported shift from 'you own' to 'commercial-use rights granted.' Do not claim the music as human-authored for copyright registration without checking the Copyright Office's AI authorship rules.
- Envato assets: register each Element download to this app as its end product, do not ship UI-kit or illustration items 'as-is' as the app's identity, never build the logo/app icon from an Envato item (cannot be trademarked), and do not use Envato music as the 'library' since redistribution as-is is prohibited.
- Tone/content: the 1.1 objectionable-content rules (and Apple's 2009 rejection of the official South Park app) mean irreverent humor must avoid targeting real groups, real people, or real corporations; keep satire aimed at invented institutions and generic human foibles.

## Open uncertainties
- Reddit could not be searched directly (blocked for the web tools; the social-listening tool returned only noise), so developer anecdotes cited here come from Apple Developer Forums, B4X, and press rather than r/iOSProgramming threads.
- Apple publishes rejection counts only by broad category ('Legal': 495,673 in 2025); there is no public per-guideline figure for 5.2.1 specifically, and the 1,177 IP removals are labeled under DPLA 6.3, not Guideline 5.2.
- Suno's ownership language is in flux: the help-center article still says paid subscribers 'are considered the owner,' while Digital Music News reports 2026 terms saying users are 'generally not considered the owner' but hold commercial-use rights. The live Terms of Service should be re-read at launch and the client should confirm whether app/game use requires any specific plan tier or attribution.
- Whether purely AI-generated Suno tracks are copyrightable at all is unresolved (Copyright Office Part 2, Jan 2025, requires human-authored expressive elements); this affects the client's ability to stop others from copying the app's music, not the client's right to use it.
- No documented 2025-2026 case was found of Apple rejecting an app specifically for 'art style inspired by' adult animation with wholly original characters; the conclusion that style alone is safe rests on copyright doctrine and the Copyright Office's statements, not on an App Review precedent. Reviewer discretion ('I'll know it when I see it') remains.
- The Envato Elements full License Terms and Prohibited Usage pages returned HTTP 403 to the fetch tool; quoted license rules come from Envato's 'how licensing works' page and the License FAQ as summarized in search results and should be verified on the live pages before relying on them (especially any rule on font embedding inside app binaries).
- Exact current registration status of each franchise mark (e.g., whether SOUTH PARK has a separate live Class 9 software registration, Reg. 4249884) could not be confirmed because Justia/USPTO aggregator pages returned 403; a direct TSDR search is recommended before naming anything.
- Nintendo v. Pocketpair (Palworld) is still pending in Tokyo District Court (hearing reported for Oct 1, 2026) and is a patent, not character-copyright, case; it is cited only as evidence that large rights holders litigate gameplay-adjacent similarity, and the sources for its 2026 status are secondary SEO sites.
