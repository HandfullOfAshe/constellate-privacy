# Doorhorn (working title): World Bible and Script Book

Document 02 of the Doorhorn product package. Version 1.0, 24 September 2026. Source of truth for concept, cast names and phases: `concepts/tournament/WINNER.md`. This document does not rename anything in that file.

**Summary.** Doorhorn is a deadpan mission-control comedy that lives in one phone and runs a household's morning launch and bedtime Re-entry through music, with a small invented crew whose jokes are sung into the cues so that nobody has to look at a screen. This document is the source of truth for the world, the cast, the tone, the household vocabulary and every recorded or sung line in version 1, written so that a voice actor, a lyricist or the developer can produce from it directly.

Sections 2 to 5 are the bible, section 6 the script book, section 7 the writing guide and section 8 the name check. The document's own voice is plain; the comedy lives inside the quoted lines.

---

## 2. The world

### Cul-de-Sac Mission Control

Cul-de-Sac Mission Control began as the county launch authority, built in a year when every county believed it would need one. It had a bunker with a long curved window, a control room of forty consoles, a tower on the ridge, a meteorology office, a countdown announcer's booth and a janitor's closet. In its entire operating history it launched one weather balloon, which came down in a parking lot, and a series of test candles that the county's annual report described as "promising." The consoles were mostly for show. The procedures were real, and Augustine Pell wrote all of them.

The budget cut took the rockets, the ridge and thirty-eight of the forty consoles. It did not take the staff, who had contracts, and it did not take the procedures, because nobody could find where they were filed except the janitor. The authority was decommissioned, renamed for the only street in the county that still had a sign, and relocated to the one asset the county could not sell: a household's kitchen counter. The bunker now lives in the phone. The launch tower rises from the counter, next to the fruit bowl, and its sky runs the whole morning: deep indigo at T-minus twelve, gold at the manifest bridge, hot orange in the final chorus and a clean white flash at liftoff. At night the sky inverts to dusk purple and lamp-glow amber for Re-entry.

Today the authority runs exactly one launch a day: yours. Its staff are the Flight Director, the Janitor, the Meteorologist, the Countdown Announcer, the CAPCOM and an abort robot. Everyone in the building is bad at their job except the janitor, and the building has never lost a crew member, because the crew is the household and the household goes out the door every morning whether or not anyone in the bunker is competent. The authority's only rival is the Department of Five More Minutes, a bureau nobody has ever visited, which issues delay permits nobody applied for and refers all complaints to its Board of Snooze Appeals, which has never convened.

### The bunker and the launch tower

The bunker is what the household sees when anyone does look at a screen: the Lock Screen and StandBy countdown, the Hangar overnight, the End Credits card and the annotated score on the first run. It is drawn in flat saturated color with hard-edged cutout characters, one silhouette and one signature color each, no paper grain, no cutout jitter, no mitten hands, so that everything survives tinted and clear icon modes.

Two consoles survived the budget cut, Flight's and CAPCOM's, side by side under the curved window. Behind the window is the kitchen: the tower rises from the counter with a gantry arm that swings away when the horns come in, and a small light at the top that is the only thing lit during Re-entry. The meteorology office is a desk with an umbrella stand; the announcer's booth is a chair, a cardigan and a chrome microphone; the janitor's closet is the only room with a labeled door; Scrub lives in the corner by the door. After splashdown the End Credits roll over the lit tower on indigo and the card hangs on the clothesline widget. Launch days show in color, scrubbed days in greyscale, and nothing in the bunker ever displays a streak.

### The Department of Five More Minutes

The Department is the recurring antagonist and it is an institution, not a person. Nobody works there on screen. It communicates entirely by permit, delivered to CAPCOM's console, and every permit grants five more minutes to a household that did not ask for them. The Department's motto, printed on every permit, is "Approved in advance."

Its Board of Snooze Appeals is the body to which Flight's denials are referred. The Board convenes "at a later date." There has never been a later date. The Board has never won a case and has never lost one, because it has never met, and it regards this as an unbroken record.

In the app: when the household takes a delay (the "+5" action), Lionel reads the permit, Augustine denies it on procedure, and the delay happens anyway, because the household is in charge and a delay is never punished. Flight logs the launch at the new time. A second delay "convenes" the Board with a three-second kazoo gavel; it adjourns without ruling. The bit never escalates. The Department never wins an argument and never loses a delay, which is the joke.

Permit language, for Lionel to read (rotate):

- "Permit received. Department of Five More Minutes. Purpose: five more minutes. Applicant: none."
- "Permit received. Approved in advance. Appeals to the Board of Snooze Appeals, convening at a later date."
- "Permit received. Permit number withheld. The Department thanks the household for its business, which it did not ask for."

### What the satire may hit

Satire in Doorhorn aims at two targets only: invented institutions (the county launch authority, the Department of Five More Minutes, the Board of Snooze Appeals) and countdown melodrama (the solemn voice, the checklist, the horns, the idea that shoes are a matter of national consequence). It never aims at real space agencies, real call signs, real broadcasters, real weather personalities, brands, companies, politicians or any real person. The crew are civil servants of a county that does not exist, and the only rocket they have ever seen came down in a parking lot.

### Augustine Pell, Flight Director

- **Role.** Runs the launch. Calls every T-minus. Owns the procedure.
- **Essence.** Any morning is survivable with a procedure.
- **Silhouette, color, prop.** Tall and narrow, a single-ear headset with a boom mic, hair in a tight dome bun, a clipboard held flat like a tray. Signal green. The laminated checklist, which she has never been seen without.
- **Voice and lane.** Level, unhurried, mid-register, no rise at the end of sentences. Brass and snare: her cues sit over a snare pattern, and the two-note brass sting is hers.
- **What she wants.** For the procedure to hold. Not for the household to be fast; for the steps to happen in order.
- **Flaw.** She believes procedure solves feelings. When something is not on the checklist she adds it to the checklist rather than dealing with it. She cannot improvise and does not try.
- **Comedic function.** The straight woman and the deadpan engine. The bunker is chaos and she narrates it as if reading a manual. Her calm is the joke.
- **How she treats the household.** As a crew she would fly with. A scrub is "a decision we made on time." Blame goes to weather or the Department, never to a person.
- **Five sample lines.**
  1. "T-minus twelve. Launch window open. Nobody has to be good at this yet."
  2. "Checklist item four: socks. There is no item five until there is a four."
  3. "The Department of Five More Minutes has sent a permit. I have sent it back. We proceed."
  4. "A scrub is a decision. We made it on time."
  5. "I have never raised my voice. I have raised a countdown."
- **Three things she never does.** She never raises her voice. She never says "hurry" or "late" as an instruction. She never names a person as the reason for anything.

### Gus Marchetti, Janitor and de facto Payload Specialist

- **Role.** Sings the manifest in the bridge. Knows where everything is. The only competent person in the building.
- **Essence.** The calm of a man who has seen every lunchbox.
- **Silhouette, color, prop.** Wide and low, round shoulders, flat cap, coveralls. Coverall blue. A key ring the size of a dinner plate at his hip; it is the only thing in the bunker that jingles.
- **Voice and lane.** Warm baritone folk. Sung, not spoken, whenever the manifest is involved; spoken only in cues and the Flight Review.
- **What he wants.** To be left to his floors, and for the household to sleep well. He would not say the second part.
- **Flaw.** He never says an item is missing. He says where it was last seen and lets you draw the conclusion. He also cannot stop noticing, which is exhausting for a man who only wanted to mop.
- **Comedic function.** Competence inside a building of incompetence. He is the emotional center and the one the household comes to trust, so his lines carry the least comedy and the most weight; the humor is in how little he needs to say.
- **How he treats the household.** As people whose things he respects. He sings their exact words for each item. He never says "forgot," never says "again," and reads a Hold item at day twenty-three with the same voice as at day one.
- **Five sample lines.**
  1. "Keys, last seen kitchen counter, by the fruit that nobody eats."
  2. "Library book, due Thursday. Thursday is today. That's all."
  3. "The good water bottle is in the dishwasher. The other water bottle is a fine water bottle."
  4. "Dentist call, day twenty-three, still on the manifest. It isn't going anywhere, and neither is the dentist."
  5. "I don't run this place. I just know where it is."
- **Three things he never does.** He never sings a judgment (no "finally," "at last," "you forgot"). He never says "still" outside the fixed Hold phrase. He never claims authority; he is not the boss and does not want to be.

### Dr. Ines Farrago, Meteorologist

- **Role.** Delivers the morning forecast in the station ID and the Request Line bits. Has forecast catastrophe every morning since 1998 and has been wrong every day.
- **Essence.** A streak worth protecting.
- **Silhouette, color, prop.** Tall pointed lab-coat collar, enormous round glasses, hair drawn as a single thundercloud shape. Storm violet. An umbrella she opens indoors and never takes outside.
- **Voice and lane.** Precise, delighted, slightly too fast. Theremin-flecked lounge: a wobble under every forecast, a vibraphone hit when she is wrong.
- **What she wants.** To protect the streak. Also, genuinely, to see some weather. Both at once.
- **Flaw.** Pride. She has never once looked out the window, on principle, because a forecast that checks is not a forecast.
- **Comedic function.** The daily wrong-in-a-new-way. She is specific, not dim: every catastrophe is an actual weather phenomenon relocated into the kitchen. She is the second character kids will draw.
- **How she treats the household.** As the audience for a great show that never arrives. When the morning turns out fine, she is pleased for them and counts it toward the streak.
- **Five sample lines.**
  1. "Fog in the hallway by seven-fifteen. Visibility: one sock."
  2. "I have been wrong every morning since 1998, and I have never once been late with it."
  3. "A cold front is moving out of the refrigerator. I advise closing the door on it."
  4. "Meteor shower over the cereal bowl. Wear a hat. Bring a spoon."
  5. "The forecast is catastrophe. The catastrophe is running late."
- **Three things she never does.** She never forecasts anything real or frightening (no actual storms, floods, fires or illness; her catastrophes are domestic and impossible). She never gets one right. She never looks out the window.

### Bunny Kowalczyk, Countdown Announcer

- **Role.** Runs Re-entry at night: tidy, teeth, book, lights, splashdown. Hosts the End Credits.
- **Essence.** A late-night voice who insists bedtime is called splashdown.
- **Silhouette, color, prop.** Always seated, leaning back, cardigan, one hand on the mic. Cream with chrome. A chrome microphone on a stand taller than the desk.
- **Voice and lane.** Late-night jazz. Low, unhurried, amused. Brushes on the snare, a walking bass that slows down as Re-entry descends.
- **What she wants.** For the day to land softly, and for the buzzer to be heard as a period at the end of a sentence rather than a punishment.
- **Flaw.** She will not say "bedtime," ever, and will restructure an entire sentence to avoid it. She loves her own voice and narrates moments that would be better silent.
- **Comedic function.** The tonal pivot. She runs the morning's phase grammar in reverse with none of the morning's urgency, and the contrast is the comedy. She closes the day.
- **How she treats the household.** As regulars at a show she has hosted for years. Nobody is in trouble; nobody has ever been in trouble at this station.
- **Five sample lines.**
  1. "Good evening, crew. This is Re-entry. Tempo is coming down, and so are we."
  2. "Teeth. Two minutes. The song knows when you're done."
  3. "Lights in a moment. Nobody's in trouble. Nobody's ever been in trouble."
  4. "Splashdown. That's the day, and it was a good one, and I'll see you at the top of the next one."
  5. "The buzzer isn't a punishment. It's a period at the end of a sentence."
- **Three things she never does.** She never says "bedtime." She never rushes a phase or counts down in numbers. She never reads a number aloud in the End Credits.

### Lionel Abara, CAPCOM

- **Role.** Capsule communicator. His entire job is to say "copy." He reads permits from the Department and closes every station ID.
- **Essence.** One word, done with total commitment, now also at home.
- **Silhouette, color, prop.** Rectangular, square-shouldered, sits very upright. Safety yellow. An oversized headset whose cord has never been untangled.
- **Voice and lane.** Dry spoken word, never sung, riding the bass stem with a short radio-squelch tail on every "copy." (WINNER.md gives Lionel no lane; this is the proposed one.)
- **What he wants.** To be useful. He has one word and he intends to earn it.
- **Flaw.** He copies everything, including things he should not agree to. He has started saying it at home, and his family has started saying it back.
- **Comedic function.** Punctuation. He is the callback machine: the last word of every ID, the button on every scene, the one-word running gag whose longest lines are confessions.
- **How he treats the household.** As fellow crew whose transmissions deserve acknowledgment. He copies the children.
- **Five sample lines.**
  1. "Copy."
  2. "Flight, this is CAPCOM. Copy. That's it. That's the transmission."
  3. "The Department of Five More Minutes is on the other line. I told them copy. I have no idea what I agreed to."
  4. "My kid asked for cereal this morning. I said copy. My kid said copy back. We're a family now."
  5. "For the record, I also say 'roger.' I've never had a reason to. I'm waiting."
- **Three things he never does.** He never contradicts Flight. He never speaks during the manifest bridge. He never says more than the bit needs; brevity is the character.

### Scrub, abort robot

- **Role.** The abort. Anyone presses Scrub; Scrub declares "weather" as the official reason; the day is logged without penalty; tomorrow's launch is shorter.
- **Essence.** A toddler-sized robot with one big button and no opinion about whose fault anything is.
- **Silhouette, color, prop.** A cube on two stubby treads, one enormous dome button on top, two dot eyes. Abort red. The button is the prop and the character. No arms, no antenna, no binocular eyes.
- **Voice and lane.** Short bursts, toy-piano pitch, kazoo blips between phrases. Every line ends within five seconds.
- **What Scrub wants.** To be pressed only when needed, and for the crew to feel fine afterward. Scrub would also like to be a meteorologist.
- **Flaw.** Scrub believes "weather" explains everything and is a little smug that its weather is always right while Dr. Farrago's never is.
- **Comedic function.** The blameless abort. The character kids will draw. The klaxon that turns a bad morning into a report.
- **How Scrub treats the household.** "Nobody did anything wrong" is in nearly every report. Scrub files no penalty, keeps no grade and says so.
- **Five sample lines.**
  1. "Weather."
  2. "Launch scrubbed. Reason: weather. Nobody's fault. Beep."
  3. "Tomorrow: shorter. Same crew. Same door."
  4. "Scrub is a button, not a grade."
  5. "Weather report: it is weather. Filed. Done."
- **Three things Scrub never does.** Scrub never gives a reason other than weather. Scrub never assigns fault. Scrub never appears unless a human presses the button.

## 4. Tone rules

1. **Deadpan, never shouty.** The crew treats a missing shoe as a matter of procedure and says so in a level voice. Nobody in the bunker ever yells, panics or pleads. Urgency comes from the music, not the cast.
2. **Ensemble warmth, workplace-family shape.** Borrow from adult-animation ensemble comedy the thing that makes it rewatchable: everyone is bad at their job and loved for it, one person is quietly competent, and the jokes come from character rather than shock. Borrow the shape, never a character, a name, a catchphrase or a roster.
3. **"Everyone shows up" stakes.** Borrow from blockbuster ensemble marketing the grammar of assembly: the Stem Roll Call is the team coming together, T-0 is the first time all stems play, silence on a stem is audible, and the launch fanfare is earned by presence. Never borrow a logo, a tagline, a title word or a color-coded roster that maps onto a real franchise.
4. **Satire hits invented institutions and countdown melodrama only.** The county launch authority, the Department of Five More Minutes, the Board of Snooze Appeals and the solemn theater of a countdown are fair targets. Real agencies, real call signs, real broadcasters, brands, companies and any real person are off limits in every line, prompt, screenshot and keyword.
5. **The crew never blames the household.** Not in a joke, not in a lyric, not by implication. Blame goes to weather or the Department. A late person is "in progress." A missing item is "last seen." A scrub is "a decision we made on time."
6. **Jokes live in lyrics and five-second cues.** At 6:58 a.m. nobody is looking at a screen, so a joke that needs a screen does not exist. Every joke must land in a sung line, a spoken cue of five seconds or less, or a sting. Longer bits are reserved for the Request Line (people are standing at a microwave), the End Credits (people are looking) and the Sunday Flight Review.
7. **Rating ceiling is 4+/9+ and it is a hard ceiling.** No profanity or minced oaths, no alcohol, tobacco or drugs, no violence or threats, no romance beyond affection, no bodily humor of any kind, no frightening weather, no medical scares, no politics, no religion, no jokes about bodies, diets, money stress or intelligence. If a line would lift the questionnaire above 9+, it does not ship.
8. **Wrong, not dumb.** Dr. Farrago is proud and specific, not a bumbling sidekick. Lionel is committed, not slow. Every character is competent at being themselves. No character exists to be laughed at for a deficiency.
9. **Numbers live in the log, never in the credits.** The mission log may say "on time 42 of 50." The End Credits show billing order and one joke and never a number, a percentage, an ordinal or a comparison, because credits that count become a scoreboard.
10. **Specific beats referential.** No pop-culture references, no quoting, no "in the style of." The comedy comes from the world's own rules (the streak, the permit, the janitor who knows where things are) and from the household's own manifest. A line that only works if you know a show is not a Doorhorn line.

## 5. Household vocabulary

The app uses these words and no synonyms. Title case as shown is the on-screen form; the crew speak them in ordinary case.

| Term | What it is | Exact wording the app uses |
|---|---|---|
| Launch | The morning run from the station ID to the door tap and fanfare. | "Launch"; "Today's launch"; "Launch at 7:28"; log entry "Launched." |
| Launch window | The span from the scheduled start to the scheduled door time, in minutes of the chosen track (8, 10, 12, 15 or 20). | "Launch window open" (spoken at start); "Launch window 7:28–7:40" (Live Activity); "Window closed" (spoken only after a scrub or after the long final chorus ends without a door tap). |
| Scrub | The abort. Anyone presses it; the launch ends; the day is logged with no penalty; tomorrow offers the next-shorter track. | Button label "Scrub"; confirmation "Scrub? Nobody's fault."; log entry "Scrubbed. Weather." |
| Weather | The official and only reason for every scrub. | "Reason: weather." The app never offers another reason and never asks for one. |
| Manifest | The household-edited list Gus sings in the bridge, with "last seen" memory. | "Manifest"; "Tonight's manifest"; "Edit manifest"; per item "last seen: kitchen counter." |
| Hold item | The one avoided task the manifest may carry. It ages one day at a time, capped at 30, and is read every morning without judgment. | "Hold item"; "On hold: dentist call, day 23"; buttons "Clear" and "Amnesty"; a manifest may hold exactly one. |
| Amnesty | Retiring the Hold item without doing it. | Button "Amnesty"; confirmation "Retire this from the manifest? No further questions."; log entry "Retired by amnesty." |
| Roll call | The Stem Roll Call in the bridge: each crew member taps their patch and their stem punches in on the next bar. | "Roll call"; "Tap your patch"; Live Activity "Roll call: 2 of 3 stems in." (numbers allowed here; this is the log surface). |
| Stem | A crew member's layer of the launch track (drums, bass, brass, choir). | "Your stem: brass"; "Stem in" on confirm. |
| Patch | A crew member's circular badge in their color; the roll-call button. | "Patch"; "Pick your patch"; "Crew patches." |
| Walk-up | The five-second personal cue that fires when a crew member's stem punches in. | "Walk-up"; "Choose your walk-up"; options are listed by flavor name only. |
| Re-entry | The evening run in reverse phase order: tidy, teeth, book, lights, splashdown, over a descending-tempo track. | "Re-entry"; "Re-entry at 8:15"; log entry "Re-entry complete." |
| Splashdown | The end of Re-entry: the buzzer and the house going dark. Bunny's word, adopted by the app. | "Splashdown"; log entry "Splashdown 8:36." The app never says "bedtime." |
| Hangar | The overnight StandBy screen: test pattern in crew colors with the tower in silhouette. | "Hangar"; "Hangar mode on in StandBy." |
| Flight review | The two-minute Sunday recap of the week's launches, scrubs and crew notes. | "Flight Review"; "Flight Review, Sunday 9:00"; "Add a crew note." |

Also in use, defined in WINNER.md and not repeated here: T-minus, station ID, the horns, the bridge, the long final chorus, Request Line, End Credits, mission log, the Department of Five More Minutes, the Board of Snooze Appeals.

After T-0 the final chorus continues for up to three minutes (the long final chorus); a door tap in that span logs as "Launched, long chorus." The product spec owns the rest.

## 6. Scripts

Conventions: spoken lines are in quotation marks; sung lines are marked (sung). Timings are wall-clock within the cue. Slots in curly braces are filled by the app from household data. All lines are original and contain no brand names.

### 6a. Station IDs (three, 30 seconds each)

The station ID is the alarm sound (30-second cap) and the first thing the house hears. It rotates daily among these three and the seasonal variants in 6j. Music: the two-note brass sting (up a fourth) over a snare, at the locked marching-band tempo.

**Station ID 1: "Standard Morning"**

- 0:00–0:02 Brass sting, two notes rising, snare roll under.
- 0:02–0:07 LIONEL: "Cul-de-Sac Mission Control. Formerly the county launch authority. Currently the kitchen."
- 0:07–0:15 AUGUSTINE: "This is Flight. We have one launch today. It is yours. The window opens when I say so, and I am about to say so."
- 0:15–0:22 GUS (sung): "Morning, crew. I know where everything is. Most of it."
- 0:22–0:26 FARRAGO: "Forecast: catastrophe. Details to follow. They will be wrong."
- 0:26–0:29 Horn tag, three notes, snare hit.
- 0:29–0:30 LIONEL: "Copy."

**Station ID 2: "Roll Call"**

- 0:00–0:03 Snare cadence, four beats; brass enters on the fifth.
- 0:03–0:09 LIONEL: "Mission Control to the cul-de-sac. Mission Control to the cul-de-sac. We know you're there. We can hear the kettle."
- 0:09–0:16 AUGUSTINE: "Crew, this is Flight. Procedure for today: wake, wash, dress, manifest, door. Same as yesterday. That's the point of a procedure."
- 0:16–0:21 BUNNY: "And I'll be back tonight for Re-entry. Sleep is also a launch. Backwards."
- 0:21–0:25 SCRUB (kazoo blip): "Scrub is here. Scrub hopes not to be needed. Beep."
- 0:25–0:28 Horn tag.
- 0:28–0:30 LIONEL: "Copy. Window opening."

**Station ID 3: "Permit Denied"**

- 0:00–0:02 Brass sting.
- 0:02–0:08 LIONEL: "Cul-de-Sac Mission Control. Incoming document. It's from the Department of Five More Minutes. It's a permit."
- 0:08–0:15 AUGUSTINE: "Nobody applied for a permit. Permit denied. Log it. The Board of Snooze Appeals may convene at a later date. There is no later date."
- 0:15–0:22 GUS (sung): "Meanwhile, the keys are on the counter, and the counter is where you left it."
- 0:22–0:26 FARRAGO: "Hail expected. Frozen peas, mostly. Freezer door."
- 0:26–0:29 Horn tag.
- 0:29–0:30 LIONEL: "Copy."

### 6b. Augustine's launch calls

Each call is under five seconds. The T-marks map to the track's section map: T-12 window open, T-10 wake and wash (the teeth song is nested here), T-8 key change and dress, T-5 manifest bridge and roll call, T-2 horns and shoes, T-0 door. Shorter tracks drop the earliest calls; the section map decides which.

**T-12 (launch window open)**

1. "T-minus twelve. Launch window open. Nobody has to be good at this yet."
2. "T-minus twelve. This is Flight. The window is open and so are the curtains, which is item one."
3. "T-minus twelve. Launch window open. The procedure begins with feet on the floor. Feet, please."

**T-10 (wake and wash)**

1. "T-minus ten. Wash phase. The teeth song is nested; when it ends, so do the teeth."
2. "T-minus ten. Faces washed or faces in progress. Both count. Proceed."
3. "T-minus ten. Wash complete or wash reported complete. I take your word for it. That's the procedure."

**T-8 (key change, dress)**

1. "T-minus eight. Key change. That's the dress phase. Socks are a pair by definition."
2. "T-minus eight. Dress phase. Dress for the door, not for the room."
3. "T-minus eight. Dress. If the shirt is inside out, it is a shirt. Proceed."

**T-5 (manifest bridge, roll call)**

1. "T-minus five. Manifest bridge. Gus has the list. Crew, tap your patch when you hear your name."
2. "T-minus five. Roll call. Silence on a stem means a crew member is not here yet. That's information, not a problem."
3. "T-minus five. Bridge. Listen to the janitor. He is the only one of us who knows where anything is."

**T-2 (horns, shoes)**

1. "T-minus two. Horns. Shoes go on when the horns come in. The horns are coming in."
2. "T-minus two. Shoes and door. The order is shoes, then door. It has never worked the other way."
3. "T-minus two. Horns. If you're looking for the other shoe, it is where the first one was."

**T-0 (door, liftoff)**

1. "T-zero. Door. Liftoff. Clean launch, crew. I have logged it."
2. "T-zero. We have a door, we have a crew, we have liftoff. Mission Control is now a kitchen again."
3. "T-zero. Liftoff. All stems present. That's what it sounds like when everybody shows up."

**Scrubbed launch (three variants, after the Scrub klaxon)**

1. "Scrub confirmed. Reason: weather. Scrub has filed it. There is no penalty; there was never a penalty. Tomorrow's launch is shorter."
2. "Launch scrubbed. This is Flight. A scrub is a decision, and we made it on time. Log it as weather. Regroup."
3. "Scrub. Copy that, Scrub. Crew, stand down. The window closes gently. We open it again tomorrow, a little closer to the door."

**Late but successful (two variants, door tap during the long final chorus)**

1. "Door. Late, and launched. The log says launched. The log doesn't have a column for the other part."
2. "T-plus three. Door. We call that a launch with a long final chorus. Logged. Go."

### 6c. Gus's manifest bridge

**Structure.** The bridge is eight bars at the flavor's locked tempo, extendable in four-bar units to sixteen. One item per bar. A "last seen" phrase takes its own bar. The Hold item takes two bars. The opener and closer are fixed and rotate from a small pool so that the household's items are the only part that changes day to day.

**Template.**


## 7. Writing guide for future lines

### 7.1 How to write a sung cue

WINNER.md locks each flavor to one tempo so that stems assemble on device; it does not print the numbers. The working values below are the ones this document assumes, and the music document owns them. If the music document changes a tempo, recompute this table before writing any new line.

| Flavor | Working BPM | Meter | Seconds per bar | Bars in a 5-second cue | Safe syllables per bar | Max syllables in a 5-second cue |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marching band | 120 | 4/4 | 2.00 | 2.5 | 4–6 | 12 (target 10) |
| Surf | 150 | 4/4 | 1.60 | 3.1 | 3–5 | 12 (target 9) |
| Disco | 118 | 4/4 | 2.03 | 2.5 | 4–6 | 12 (target 10) |
| Big band (swing) | 132 | 4/4 | 1.82 | 2.7 | 4–5 | 11 (target 9) |
| Bluegrass | 140 | 4/4 (cut-time feel) | 1.71 | 2.9 | 3–5 | 11 (target 9) |
| Lullaby / Re-entry | 84 descending to 64 | 4/4 | 2.86 to 3.75 | 1.7 to 1.3 | 5–8 | 12 (target 8) |
| Station ID and fanfare | 120 | 4/4 | 2.00 | 2.5 | 4–6 | 12 (target 10) |

Rules:

1. **One and a half syllables per beat is the ceiling** for intelligibility at 6:58 a.m.; the safe column is lower on purpose. A cue that needs more syllables needs more bars, not faster singing.
2. **A cue starts on a downbeat and ends before the next phase marker.** Phase changes are quantized to bar boundaries, so a long cue is cut by the engine, not trimmed by the singer. Write to the bar count, then read it against a metronome at the locked tempo.
3. **Manifest items get one bar each.** An item over the safe count is split at a natural break ("the good / water bottle") or loses its article; a "last seen" phrase gets its own bar; the Hold line gets two.
4. **Stress falls on the item, never on "the" or "and."** Sung lines never end on "you"; they end on the item, the place or the fixed closer.
5. **Spoken cues over music allow two syllables per beat.** Augustine's calls run 10 to 14 syllables inside 2.5 bars at 120. Rhymes are optional and never forced; the manifest does not rhyme because the household's words are not negotiable.

### 7.2 How to keep Gus neutral

Gus reads the manifest exactly as filed. The household's wording is the lyric. The following words never appear in a Gus line, in any position: again, finally, at last, yet, already, forgot, forgotten, forget, remember, should, need to, don't, always, never (about a person), even, just (as in "just do it"), late, hurry. "Still" appears only inside the fixed Hold phrase. "Your" is allowed for possession ("your keys") and never for fault ("your fault," "your problem"). Gus states location and time as facts ("last seen the couch," "due Thursday") and adds nothing. When a fact is funny on its own ("keys, last seen the hook, imagine that"), the tag is about the object, not the person. A Gus line that would make a listener feel caught is rewritten until it would make them feel found.

### 7.3 How to write for a 4+/9+ rating

- No profanity, no minced oaths, no substitutions that imply one.
- No alcohol, tobacco, drugs, gambling or medication as a subject, even in passing ("the wine," "the pills" do not appear in any line; a Hold item entered by the household is read verbatim, and the app's own writing never introduces these).
- No violence, threats, weapons or injury. Farrago's catastrophes are impossible and domestic: fog in the hallway, hail of peas. Never a real hazard, never an illness, never anything a child could believe.
- No romance beyond affection; no jokes about bodies, weight, food choices, intelligence, age, money, gender, accents or family structure.
- No bodily humor of any kind.
- No politics, religion, real events, real people, real companies, real agencies or real call signs.
- No scary sounds: the Scrub klaxon is a toy klaxon, and the Re-entry buzzer is a game-show buzzer at low volume.
- Every character is someone a nine-year-old could draw and a four-year-old could like.

### 7.4 Checklist to run before any new line ships

1. Does it fit in its slot at the locked tempo (7.1), read aloud against a metronome?
2. Is it under five seconds unless it is a Request Line bit, a credits line or Flight Review?
3. Does it blame anyone, directly or by implication? If yes, rewrite so that weather or the Department carries the blame, or cut.
4. Does it contain a banned word from 7.2 (for Gus) or a rating breach from 7.3 (for anyone)?
5. Does it reference any real person, brand, company, agency, call sign, show, film, song, artist or catchphrase? If yes, cut; there is no rewrite.
6. Is the joke specific to the world's rules or the household's manifest, rather than a reference?
7. Is it the character's voice: Augustine's checklist, Gus's facts, Farrago's pride, Bunny's cool, Lionel's one word, Scrub's bursts?
8. If it is a credits line: does it contain a number, ordinal, percentage or comparison? If yes, cut.
9. If it is a Farrago forecast: is it wrong in a way not already used in 6d, two sentences, impossible and harmless?
10. Has it been read aloud in a kitchen at speaking volume with a kettle running? If it did not land, it does not ship.
11. Has the household's reluctant partner heard it and not winced?
12. Is it registered in the lyric and script collection with a date and the author's name?
